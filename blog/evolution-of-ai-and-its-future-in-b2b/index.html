Evolution of AI, and its future in B2B

Size matters. How you use it matters more. But first, let's zoom out.

When I was young, I grew up watching Doordarshan. Every Saturday evening, our family sat together to watch Jai Hanuman, and we woke up to Chitrahar on Sundays. When the signal was noisy, one of us (it was always me) went upstairs, climbed onto the roof and adjusted the antenna. A Yagi–Uda antenna, for the electronics geek. No remote, one channel, a few knobs and a twisted piece of metal on the roof.

Fast-forward twenty-five years. Each family member can watch the show they want, and any one of them can become a creator. A new release announces itself through a device vibrating on the desk. It knows something about what you want and when you want it. It fits in your pocket. You can talk to a larger community watching the same thing, live, with Super Chats. Yes, YouTube.

TV became YouTube. Radio became Spotify. We stopped flipping horizontally through pages about celebrities and started scrolling vertically through the carefully presented lives of our own friends and family. Yes, Instagram. Broadcasting, radio and print continued alongside them. The change reaches our habits and our thinking. Twenty-five years of technological evolution brought societal evolution with it. Now we have iPad babies, and children whose social accounts exist before they are born. You get the gist.

This is not merely the journey from Pentium to the M4 Singularity Blaster Pro Max. Nor is it only the journey from a computer in a garage to Google. It is the journey from the horizontal flip to the vertical scroll: different ways of finding something, paying attention to it and participating in it.

Television ratings acquired neighbours in engagement and reach. Those metrics helped organise targeted advertising around attention. A new interface became part of a different economy. That is the level at which I want to look at AI's future in business.

I first wrote this argument in May 2024. The voice-platform and efficiency forecasts below belong to that outlook; the question behind them is what a business would need before it could trust AI to become its front desk.

The front desk

Software has made many things easier while giving us another thing to learn: its interface. Moving from television to YouTube reduced the need for buttons on a physical device but introduced menus, search, subscriptions and settings on a screen. What if the next step let the customer begin with what they wanted, without first learning how the business had arranged its software?

The best UI is No UI, taken as a direction rather than a ban on screens. An AI front desk, if you will. You say what you need. It understands enough about you, the service and the situation to help you get it. The film Her offered a familiar image of that conversational entrance. Voice is one possibility; accessibility and the task still determine which other ways of interacting the person needs.

This is the front desk your customers encounter. The rest of the business works to empower it. Only now it can be hosted on the internet and reached wherever the customer is.

Distribution could change with it. We reached services through browsers, then app stores. I imagined mobile operating systems becoming large voice-assistant platforms, with apps turning into capabilities those assistants could discover and invoke. Your front desk would be distributed as one of those capabilities. Or something to that effect. I do not have a crystal ball, and my psychic powers were never strong enough to resolve the details.

Distribution and retention are two of a business's enduring problems. Getting into a new distribution channel early could matter, although it would leave businesses with new questions about who controls access to their customers. Retention is where the front desk becomes particularly interesting: it can carry the continuing relationship, rather than just help somebody find the entrance.

Size matters. How you use it matters more

Repeat after me: simply making the model bigger is not how this works.

Think about a process full of conditions and loops. It repeats, but each customer's situation needs a personal adjustment. It depends on people joining and leaving the organisation, remembering exceptions and maintaining the automation. You can end up with a million combinations to develop and maintain, and still make the customer feel like an awkward exception to a form.

AI offers an automation of some of that automation: a way to interpret variation without someone spelling out every possible conversation beforehand. Retention is expensive, risky and continuous. This is the work I expected the front desk to scale first.

A plausible conversation is the beginning. Confidence in putting it in front of customers needs several other things to develop together.

First, teach it when to shut up

A system that is good at talking also needs to know what it should not say, what it does not know and when somebody else needs to decide. Part of that is controllability; part concerns the model and architecture through which the work happens.

In 2024, I expected reinforcement learning and better planning to change what those architectures could do. The surrounding application still has to decide what the system may read, promise and change, and check that it honours those rules.

Think of an operating system. Agent orchestration arranges work somewhat like threads; tools give those activities ways to interact with the world. The growing number of tools makes creating the first demonstration easier. Choosing among them can become a task in itself.

Then the demonstration meets production. Guardrails and checks have to become more precise and keep up as customer interactions grow. A rule that worked for one prepared example has to encounter the unexpected request, the ambiguous instruction and the customer whose circumstances have changed. Scaling the useful control is harder than adding another conversation window.

Consider an invented exchange. A customer says, “Move my delivery to the address I used last time.” There are two recent addresses, and the parcel is already in transit. The front desk needs to ask which address, establish what the carrier still permits and distinguish requesting the change from confirming it. A fluent “Done” would be the easiest answer to generate and the wrong promise to make.

The useful interface might be a conversation followed by a visible card showing the parcel, chosen address and “Request the change.” The person can inspect the proposed change without first reconstructing the business's internal navigation.

Performance-review your front desk

You do not hand out bonuses without looking at the work. Nor do you fire the people who are doing it well. You assess, talk, explain what needs to improve and give people a way to improve it.

The AI front desk needs an equivalent route for examining its work. This is partly an explainability problem. The bigger practical problem is explaining it to the person in the organisation who understands front-desk operations. You would not ask the sales team to become the engineering team before they could tell you that a customer received the wrong promise.

That is why I called it an HR problem as well as a technical one. A trace can help an engineer find a failing step. The operator needs to recognise the business meaning: which policy supports the answer, what confused the system and where their correction belongs. A large log is not automatically that explanation. Nor is a model's fluent account of its own reasoning a verified report of what caused the answer.

The delivery example gives the operator a useful question. Did the system use an old address, misunderstand “last time” or promise something the carrier could not do? Those failures call for different improvements. The correction might change a source, add a check or preserve an unresolved carrier response. It need not begin with retraining the entire model.

The ambition was to isolate what was confusing the front desk, reinforce useful behaviour and correct the failure without losing your hair or waiting for hell to freeze over. That also gives a better answer to “What exactly do we want to control?” Following a disputed answer through its work makes the distinction concrete; choosing an improved version gives the correction somewhere to go.

The 8086 era, waiting for the Pentiums

The computer analogy extends to efficiency. There was a time when kilobytes of memory were an extraordinary constraint. The software we can afford to run changed as the machines and the methods improved. I expected AI to go through a similarly consequential expansion.

My 2024 illustration was deliberately large: a thousandfold efficiency improvement could, under a simple proportional model, pay for a model a hundred times larger while using a tenth of the compute. The arithmetic is 100 / 1,000 = 0.1.

Note

The illustration's assumption. Cost scales directly with model size in this calculation. Actual training and serving costs depend on much more than parameter count.

I expected larger models to bring stronger reasoning and other useful capabilities. Scaling research supplied reasons to take increases in model size, data and compute seriously, but it did not promise that every desired capability would appear at a particular size. It also made clear that the allocation among those inputs matters.1 Bigger and more efficient need not be opposing directions; neither makes control and explanation optional.

That capability gain would become useful to businesses if controllability and transparency improved alongside it. Otherwise we would have a more capable front desk whose work we still could not reliably supervise.

Your back office has to teach the front desk

Every time the organisation fails to teach the front desk what it has learned, useful domain knowledge stays out of the system representing it.

The people handling customers know why the ordinary procedure sometimes fails. They know which exception the manual misses and why a technically correct answer may be useless in practice. The front desk needs a way to receive that knowledge and use it in the next interaction.

This is why the workflows inside the organisation have to become AI-first before the whole business can. That means reorganising the work so a system can participate between the input and the person who refines its output. The back office gains a useful tool while teaching the eventual customer-facing front desk.

YojnAI approached that through agents and checked playbooks, with intermediate work, traces and feedback. The useful loop was more than collecting a thumbs-down. A person had to be able to see a mistake, explain it and have the correction reach the relevant source or operation. The organisation around the application supplies much of what makes that contribution meaningful.

Suppose local delivery restrictions keep invalidating the front desk's promises. Another preferred-answer example might hide the pattern. A maintained source of those restrictions, used at the right step, could change the work itself. The next customer then benefits from what the previous interaction taught the business.

That is how the front desk can become more than a retention engine. It can give the business a way to learn from its relationships and make more of them possible.

What about the contracts?

Believe it or not, compliance is a bitch. Making the interface pleasant does not make the contracts go away.

A business still needs to know which information may be used, what the service is permitted to promise, who can act for the customer and what the parties have actually agreed. Installing the model on a customer's infrastructure does not answer all of those questions. The data path may still involve hosted gateways, diagnostic records and feedback elsewhere.

These arrangements shape the product. A customer can ask for a delivery change without having granted permission to use the conversation for an unrelated purpose. A technically possible action can still sit outside the service the company agreed to provide. The front desk has to work within those relationships, rather than leave someone to discover them after it has made the promise.

The confidence to put it in front

Creating a minimal front desk is easy compared with trusting it to represent the business. The five parts belong together: control over what it does, explanations the operator can use, affordable capability, learning from the organisation and agreements that support the actual operation.

The possibility is a business less dependent on somebody being at a particular desk at a particular hour, without losing the knowledge those people contribute. A customer gets a simpler entrance; the business gets a way to make its experience useful at a larger scale. That is the magic worth pursuing.

How soon do you want it? What would make you confident enough to get there? Television, radio and magazines took decades to acquire the forms we now carry in our pockets. At the time I wrote this, the conversational systems behind these front desks had only just become widely accessible. The analogy does not give us a timetable.

I was the one on the roof, turning the antenna. The next change may reach us through a much less visible adjustment.

Objects in the mirror are closer than they appear. ;)
