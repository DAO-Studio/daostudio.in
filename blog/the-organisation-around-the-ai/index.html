The organisation around the AI

On this page

  2. The knowledge needs a continuing home
  3. Learning to use the capability

A business can be enthusiastic about AI and still have no settled task for it. “Help us answer customers” sounds specific until a customer asks for a promise the business has never defined. The software needs information, but the missing thing may be an agreement among the people who will use it. A model can make that absence visible. It cannot decide on the business's behalf what the agreement ought to be.

DAO Studio's ambition to make intelligence more accessible included small businesses whose knowledge did not necessarily live in a tidy system. A shopkeeper might hold the important distinctions in conversation, memory and practice. Making that knowledge usable by an application is partly a technical task and partly a relationship with the people who know why the work happens as it does. A simpler interface should bring them closer to that work, with assisted setup, a place to supply context and a correction that reaches the application. Making AI accessible included giving those people a practical contribution, rather than asking them to become software specialists first.

The tier-2 and tier-3 business context mattered to that ambition. An application designed around a dedicated administrator can ask a small business to create a job it cannot sustain. The person updating a service rule may also be serving customers and arranging tomorrow's work. A first application has to fit that day. Assisted setup, familiar examples and a short correction path may matter more than exposing every configurable feature on the first screen.

The studio’s early work considered small businesses and women in tier-2 and tier-3 India. It connected convenience and interoperability with consent, trust and the burden of maintaining configuration. It also recognised an adoption problem: contributors wanted a useful network, while a useful network needed people willing to join.

A repair shop asks for a booking assistant

Imagine a repair shop that wants an assistant to answer messages and arrange appointments. The opening task seems straightforward: identify the appliance, collect the address and offer a slot. Then a customer asks whether a particular repair will be covered, whether a substitute part is acceptable and whether the quoted price is final. The shop has several informal ways of handling those questions, depending on who is working and what is found on inspection.

An assistant could retrieve the address successfully and still make the appointment worse by giving a firm answer where the shop needs an inspection. It could also refuse every useful question, leaving the staff to repeat the whole conversation. The boundary lies in the work itself: which information can be collected, which statements can be supported now and which decisions become possible only after someone examines the appliance. The business needs a way for the conversation to reach that next stage without losing what has already been learned.

This does not require a comprehensive process manual before any experiment begins. The shop could start with intake: collect the details a technician needs and prepare a concise request for confirmation. That narrow task reveals what is missing in actual use. Staff may discover that they repeatedly need a photograph of a label, that two service areas have different travel arrangements or that the same word means different faults to different customers. Each discovery can refine the intake rather than turn into another ad hoc correction after a promise has already been sent.

Tip

Try the handoff with people first. Before handing intake to an assistant, the shop can try the sequence with its own staff. The person taking the request collects the appliance model, fault description and address, asks for a label photograph when the model is unclear, then passes that account to the person arranging the visit. The technician can reserve the diagnosis for inspection without making the initial conversation useless. A completed intake is something the next person can continue from, not merely a conversation that has ended.

Willingness to try a tool is different from having the surrounding work ready for it. Readiness belongs to the task and the people carrying it. A business may be ready for assisted intake while remaining unready for automated diagnosis or price commitments. Labelling the whole organisation mature or immature obscures the starting point.

The knowledge needs a continuing home

The shop's opening information can come from records, staff conversations and examples of past work. Those sources serve different purposes. A completed invoice can show what happened in one case without becoming the price rule for every future case. A technician's explanation can reveal an important exception without necessarily being the shop's agreed policy. The application needs the distinctions that let it use the information appropriately, not simply a larger pile of text.

Once that information is usable, somebody still has to keep it current. Service areas change. Staff availability changes. A supplier stops carrying a part. A correction in one conversation needs to reach the applicable knowledge or procedure if it is to help the next customer. Otherwise the assistant can sound increasingly experienced while repeating an obsolete promise. Maintenance is part of making the capability accessible: the people closest to the work need a practical way to notice and repair a mismatch.

Suppose the label photograph repeatedly arrives after the technician has already called the customer back. Staff can change the intake to request it earlier for the affected appliance types. Trying the revised sequence shows whether the extra request saves a call or simply adds friction for customers whose model was already clear. When an assistant takes over that step, the changed rule, its examples and the way staff correct a bad request need to travel together. The useful improvement is less reconstruction at the handoff; a larger number of automatically answered messages would not establish it.

This maintenance has an organisational shape: who knows the rule, who can change it and who notices when the application stops matching the work? The voluntary 2023 NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 treats defined tasks, roles and ongoing monitoring as part of understanding AI use. In the shop, those questions are ordinary operating questions. A good model evaluation cannot tell us whether a changed service area will reach the assistant before the next booking.

Learning to use the capability

There is a distribution problem here as well as an interface problem. The people who could benefit may not be looking for an agent platform. They may be looking for fewer unanswered enquiries or less time spent assembling the same information. A demonstration needs to meet that recognised problem, with somebody able to help translate the local practice into the application. The investment is wasted if every improvement still has to pass through that specialist forever.

A configured application and a self-service product make different demands on their users. In the first arrangement, a delivery team can help translate the business's practice into the system and carry some of the setup work. In the second, the product needs to make more of that translation possible without the team standing beside it. Hiding configuration fields does not remove the decisions behind them; it changes how those decisions have to be explained and supported.

This is where community and learning can have a substantive role. People need examples that help them recognise a suitable task, understand a result and correct the knowledge the application relies on. They also need to be able to stop a poorly fitted use without feeling that they have failed to be modern. The aim is a capability they can own in practice, not a permanent dependence on a specialist who alone understands what the system is doing.

The measures should follow that aim. A hundred conversations may be valuable or may be a hundred unfinished requests. The shop can examine whether intake arrives complete, how much correction the staff must do, whether customers receive supportable commitments and whether the resulting appointment can actually proceed. These observations make the trial useful even if the conclusion is to narrow the task, change the process or leave a particular judgment with the technician.

The repair shop can make its conversational entrance useful by letting the intake develop alongside its own work. The technician receives enough context to continue, the customer gets commitments the shop can support and a correction has somewhere to go. YojnAI approached the application through capabilities and playbooks; the organisation supplies much of what gives that composition meaning. Making AI more accessible includes making this relationship workable: a useful task, understandable output, a place for exceptions and people able to improve the arrangement as they learn.
