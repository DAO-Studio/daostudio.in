# The cases that are still waiting

A reviewer has examined the image and called the person responsible for the property. The call remains unanswered. At the end of the shift, the case is still waiting, but the report wants a result: success, failure or false alarm. Which box should the reviewer choose?

The report is asking for a completed judgment before the service has obtained one. The missing answer belongs to work somebody may still finish. An event the process has no way to handle needs a different intervention. [YOJN Care](https://daostudio.in/yojn-care/) brings these questions together: where did the response stop, and what would let it continue? Tidying the report must not erase the work.

<a id="a-pending-case-still-holds-a-question"></a>

## A pending case still holds a question

Until the contact replies, the service can preserve what the reviewer has already established alongside what still needs confirmation. Counting the case as a false alarm makes that missing confirmation disappear; counting it as a completed response makes the service look more effective than the record supports. The pending state needs to carry the question still open and a path through which it might be resolved. It is temporary uncertainty about a particular case, rather than a permanent bin for whatever the reporting system finds inconvenient.

Process noise asks another question: did something happen outside the categories or assumptions used to describe the workflow? That can reveal a missing branch, a failed process or an inadequate model of the operation. A pending confirmation might be resolved through an existing contact path. An event that no branch can describe might require a change to the process itself. Preserving the difference lets the service improve the right thing instead of asking a reviewer to force both into a final label.

<a id="a-catalogue-is-a-way-to-examine-the-design"></a>

## A catalogue is a way to examine the design

Work through the combinations of observations and response routes, and the ordinary flow soon branches. An image is incomplete. The owner cannot be reached. A reviewer and a later validator disagree. The event changes while somebody is handling it. A catalogue of such cases asks what the service can know and do at each point, including the paths an ordinary-case diagram might leave out.

The catalogue's value lies in coverage of possibilities. Ten designed cases for one outcome and ten for another can help expose different decisions without implying that the outcomes occur equally often. Those proportions describe how we constructed the catalogue. Field observations would answer the separate question of frequency. Keeping that distinction visible lets the design explore cases freely without turning its assumed distribution into a reported operating rate.

<a id="the-original-buckets-kept-that-distinction"></a>

## Which cases are we counting?

Some cases reach authorities; some are forwarded to the community as crime; some look suspicious and are later judged otherwise. These groups overlap. A case forwarded as crime and subsequently validated as crime belongs both to the forwarded-crime group and to all completed outcomes. Dividing by either group gives a meaningful fraction, but answers a different question. Cases still awaiting that judgment remain outside both completed groups until an answer arrives.

<a id="care-case-groups"></a>

### Examine a case group

Let $V$ be the number forwarded as crime and validated as crime, $C$ the number forwarded as crime whose final validation is complete, and $N$ the number of completed outcomes. Then $V/C$ asks what share of forwarded crime labels agreed with validation; $V/N$ asks what share of all completed outcomes took that confirmed-crime path. The comparison below keeps track of the cases counted in each term.

![Validated crime cases sit inside completed forwarded-crime cases and all completed outcomes, while pending and other unresolved events remain in the full recorded population.](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/the-cases-that-are-still-waiting/case-populations-light.svg)

The boundaries show which cases belong inside each population, not their relative numbers.

| Comparison | Denominator | What it asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Validated crime among completed forwarded-crime cases | Forwarded-crime cases with final validation | How often did this particular label agree with validation? |
| The same validated-crime group among completed outcomes | All completed outcomes | How much of the completed work took this path? |
| Cases awaiting final validation | All recorded events | How much recorded work is awaiting final validation? |

For unfinished work, let $P$ count cases awaiting final validation and $R$ count every recorded event once in the same reporting window. Then $P/R$ gives the pending share. $R$ includes completed outcomes, pending judgments and unresolved process exceptions; it is not silently replaced by $N+P$. An exception can still need process repair even when it is not waiting for the particular validation that defines $P$.

Understating a crime as suspicion creates a different problem from repeatedly asking people to examine false alarms. Both deserve investigation. Keeping them separate lets the service ask where it missed a serious event and where it created unnecessary work, without expecting one percentage to explain both.

<a id="the-denominator-changes-the-answer"></a>

## The denominator changes the answer

Imagine a service with 100 recorded events:

| State at the reporting time | Events | Share of all recorded events |
| --- | --: | --: |
| Resolved | 60 | 60% |
| Pending | 20 | 20% |
| Process exception | 20 | 20% |

“Resolved” identifies a completed judgment, not sixty successful responses. Agreement with a later validator could be calculated within those sixty cases, if the relevant judgments exist. It would not describe the other forty. Treating the twenty pending cases as failures would instead turn an unfinished judgment into a final one for the convenience of calculation.

The denominator follows the question. A comparison among completed reviews can describe agreement with validation. A view across all recorded events shows how much remains unresolved and how often the process encountered an exception. Keeping these questions recognisable makes it possible to examine good completed work alongside a growing backlog, instead of allowing one attractive success rate to stand for both.

<a id="where-the-delay-develops"></a>

## Where the delay develops

A correct detection can wait in a queue. A reviewer can receive incomplete evidence. A handoff can lose the context needed for the next decision. The last person attached to the case is not necessarily the cause of its delay or outcome. The record needs enough of the sequence to distinguish when the evidence became available, when work was accepted and where the next action stalled. Otherwise the score measures where the case ended up rather than what the person did.

Consider two reviewers in a constructed comparison. One receives a clear view and reaches a judgment quickly. The other receives an old frame, requests a new observation and leaves the case pending. A speed-only ranking rewards the first outcome without describing the different work. A ranking that treats every pending case as incompetence penalises the second reviewer for preserving a question the evidence could not answer. Neither comparison tells us whether a reviewer made good use of what was available. That requires the conditions and the judgment to be examined together.

Look at the pattern before blaming the last person attached to a case. Consistent failure across the service calls for examining its rules; intermittent system-wide failure may point to how work is distributed. Problems across several teams can call for training, while a local cluster may reveal a staffing gap. Individual handling still matters, but it has to be examined within those conditions.

A client's classification may be the agreed operational reference, but it is still a judgment made from a particular position. A later answer may have more context than the reviewer had, or may describe the whole incident while the initial label described one observation. Disagreement can reveal a real mistake, a changed event or a mismatched question. Looking at the evidence available at each point helps distinguish them without making either participant automatically correct.

<a id="what-a-score-teaches-the-reviewer-to-do"></a>

## What a score teaches the reviewer to do

Evaluation changes the work when it becomes an incentive. Suppose completed cases earn a reward while unresolved cases count against a reviewer. That arrangement creates a reason to finalise a label before the missing confirmation arrives. If agreement with a later client judgment is the only rewarded result, guessing the expected answer may become more attractive than describing uncertainty accurately.

Holmström and Milgrom's [multitask incentive analysis](https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24) examines how rewards for measurable work can redirect attention away from other valuable duties that are harder to measure. Its formal model is not a ready-made compensation policy for a response service. The relevant question is what a score encourages the reviewer to neglect. Speed, careful interpretation, context gathering and a reliable handoff can compete for the same attention even though the service needs them together.

Adding more scores does not automatically repair the incentive. A count of evidence requests could reward unnecessary requests. A penalty for disagreement could discourage challenge to a poor reference judgment. The comparison needs an account of good handling before its numbers acquire consequences. Reviewing a sample of completed, pending and disputed cases can help expose what the measures are missing; the review itself needs enough independence and context to avoid simply reproducing the original label.

Accountability remains possible within this richer account. A reviewer can ignore available evidence, leave an accepted task unattended or fail to make a required handoff. Naming the actual failure makes feedback more actionable than a general penalty for a disappointing outcome. It also leaves room for service-level failures to be corrected as service-level failures. Good individual decisions still depend on [capacity and recurring work across the service](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/the-cost-of-continuing-response/); a performance score cannot supply the missing shift or maintain the equipment carrying the evidence.

<a id="a-changing-service-changes-the-comparison"></a>

## A changing service changes the comparison

A revised detector changes which events enter the workflow. A revised procedure can change what a label means. A different staffing arrangement can change handling time. A before-and-after comparison that omits those changes may attribute improvement to the wrong cause, especially if the easier cases enter one period and the difficult cases another. The conditions under which the result was obtained are part of what makes the result interpretable.

The point of evaluation is to make the service easier to understand and improve. A useful account can show a reviewer making a sound judgment, a connection preventing review and an unanswered confirmation in the same operation. Those distinctions keep uncertainty available for work instead of silently converting it into achievement or error. Pending then becomes a question someone can pursue, rather than noise the report learns to hide.
