# The cost of continuing response

An alert can be inexpensive to produce and expensive to resolve. Once a service promises that somebody will examine the event, obtain missing context and carry a response forward, its cost extends beyond the notification. The continuing work is part of the product. If the business model counts only the software transaction, it can make a growing service appear healthier while the people behind it absorb an increasing burden.

[YOJN Care](https://daostudio.in/yojn-care/)’s ambition was a more affordable response service for homes, shops and neighbourhoods, combining detection with human review and community or authorised response routes. Its economics therefore depended on the relationship between monitored sites, customers and the people carrying the queue. More cameras meant more possible evidence sources. More subscriptions meant more commercial commitments. More active events meant more work now. Those counts were connected, but none could substitute for the others in an account of capacity or revenue.

<a id="staffing-grows-in-whole-steps"></a>

## Staffing grows in whole steps

The integrated service models behind Care budgeted one operator per 100 paying Tier 2 customers, rounded up, then one manager per ten operators, also rounded up. These ratios counted paying subscriptions. The handling capacity still depended on when events arrived and how much work each required. At 100 customers the formula gives one operator; at 101 it gives two. At 1,000 it gives ten operators and one manager; at 1,001 it gives eleven operators and two managers.

![Budgeted operator and manager counts rise in whole steps as paying Tier 2 customers increase.](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/the-cost-of-continuing-response/staffing-steps.svg)

Crossing a staffing threshold adds a whole person to the budget.

A model with that shape cannot spread its people costs as though every additional customer buys an infinitesimal fraction of a new shift. Equipment, desks, electricity, connectivity and staff welfare were also part of the design. Purchases associated with additional staff differ from recurring pay and from the use-driven costs of calls or transferred media.

The models also explored public funding and distribution through associations, alongside paying tiers. Those were proposed routes to making coverage affordable, not committed funding or authorised participation by any public body or association. A low subscription price still needed a credible account of who paid for the rest of the operation.

<a id="twenty-cases-in-an-hour"></a>

## Twenty cases in an hour

Consider an illustrative service receiving twenty cases in an hour, with six minutes of active handling per case. That creates 120 person-minutes of work: the equivalent of two people working continuously for the hour. It says nothing yet about breaks, shift changes, unexpected complexity or the speed at which a new case can begin. Nor does it establish that two people are adequate for the service's response commitments.

The arrival pattern can change the experience without changing the total. Give the toy service two reviewers, assume every case takes exactly six uninterrupted minutes, and omit handoffs and all other work. The contrast is already substantial:

| Arrival pattern | First cases begin | Last case begins | Waiting before active handling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All twenty arrive at minute zero | Immediately, two at a time | Minute 54 | Last pair waits 54 minutes |
| One arrives every three minutes | Immediately | On its arrival at minute 57 | None under these simplified assumptions |

Both patterns require 120 person-minutes. In the burst, the reviewers complete ten successive pairs; the last pair cannot start until nine six-minute intervals have passed. The workload total alone says nothing about that wait.

Handling time itself has several forms. Examining an image consumes attention. Waiting for a contact to answer may consume less attention but leaves a case open and creates work when the answer arrives. A handoff may involve two people for part of the interval. Counting every open case as continuous labour overstates one kind of load; counting only button presses erases another. The operation needs to distinguish elapsed case time, active work and the number of cases a person can carry coherently.

Little's [queueing result](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/opre.9.3.383) relates long-run average number in a system, arrival rate and time in the system under its conditions. It helps explain how work accumulates. A deadline asks a further question about the distribution of waiting, the response arrangement and the consequences of delay, not only its average.

<a id="the-next-customer-may-require-a-whole-new-step"></a>

## The next customer may require a whole new step

Some costs grow with use: media transfer, storage, calls or other consumed services. Others arrive in steps. Additional shift coverage requires people even before the new capacity is fully used. A new site may require an installation, device configuration and someone able to maintain it. Additional operating space or equipment creates a different cash requirement from a monthly cloud bill.

This produces a changing relationship between scale and cost. A service with spare capacity may be able to carry more customers without immediately adding a shift. At a capacity boundary, the next group of customers may require a substantial addition. Spreading all costs smoothly across each subscriber can conceal that step. Treating initial equipment and setup as though they recur identically every month creates a different error. A useful economic model distinguishes the work and the timing of the expenditure before averaging it into a unit cost.

Revenue has its own units and timing. An installed site is not necessarily a paying subscriber, and a payer may cover several monitored sources. Acquisition can bring people into the service without converting them into the recurring revenue expected by the model. Cash may arrive after the installation and staffing cost has already been incurred. Growth can improve utilisation while increasing the money needed to keep the service running through the transition.

<a id="acquisition-and-response-are-different-operations"></a>

## Acquisition and response are different operations

A distributed partner network can help reach and install more customers. That is a route to acquisition, not an automatic multiplication of response capacity. A partner able to onboard several sites a day may have no role in reviewing their events. If demand is pooled centrally, the shared queue must absorb the new work. If response is local, coverage and escalation need to remain viable in each local operation. Neither arrangement becomes sustainable merely because the onboarding numbers are strong.

The confusion is easy to build into a model. Divide a customer target by the number of customers onboarded per day and the result is a number of **days**, not operators; manager and salary lines calculated from it cannot show the capacity or surplus their labels suggest. A falling cost per customer needs the same care: a cost worked out for one number of paying users cannot be carried into a column that assumes another.

There is also recurring work between incidents: checking contact details, maintaining equipment, correcting configuration, training people and examining failures. Omitting it from the model treats continuity as free. Moving it to the customer may lower the provider's cost while changing who has to do the work and how dependable the service is. That is a product choice with consequences for use, not just a favourable change in a spreadsheet.

Automation can reduce a specific part of this burden. Better evidence grouping may save repeated review; a reliable routine notification may save manual contact. But a detector that produces more candidate events can increase downstream work even while its computation becomes cheaper. The useful measure is the work required to complete the response at the intended quality, including exceptions, rather than the cost of the automated stage alone.

The economic question is ultimately whether the service can keep its commitment under the conditions in which customers use it. [Unfinished reviews](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/the-cases-that-are-still-waiting/) still need capacity, and [the path carrying their evidence](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/the-evidence-path/) still needs maintenance. A credible model gives that recurring work a place in the cost of continuing. Growth can then be examined for the response the operation can sustain, including the labour and spare capacity on which its promise depends.
