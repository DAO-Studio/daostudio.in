# Who carries the incident?

Imagine a person appearing in a camera frame after a shop's usual hours. The system raises an alert, a neighbour opens it and a monitoring operator receives the same event. Who is carrying it now? The neighbour may assume the operator will act. The operator may be waiting for the owner to explain the scene. The notification has travelled successfully while the responsibility remains between people.

This is the problem behind [YOJN Care](https://daostudio.in/yojn-care/): how to keep an incident together as different people try to make sense of it and decide what to do. A better detection can help them begin. It cannot tell the next operator what the owner said, whether a neighbour agreed to investigate or who is still waiting for an answer.

<a id="an-alert-needs-the-knowledge-people-hold-about-the-scene"></a>

## An alert needs the knowledge people hold about the scene

The owner might recognise authorised staff where the neighbour sees an unfamiliar person. The operator may have neither person's knowledge. A reference view, the property's instructions and other observations give them something to judge together. The person in the frame has not changed; what they know about that person has.

A personal SOS starts somewhere else. Somebody has deliberately asked for help. They should not need a camera to corroborate the request before it becomes worth attending to. Keeping that origin visible tells the reviewer what question they are answering.

<a id="notifications-have-to-become-an-incident-somebody-can-follow"></a>

## Notifications have to become an incident somebody can follow

Two cameras might show the same person, and each might send several frames as the person moves. Treating every notification as a new case makes more work while scattering the evidence needed to understand any one of them. Group them, and the reviewer can see what changed. A judgment on the first frame can sit beside a later observation still needing attention.

The same context matters when the shift changes. The incoming operator needs the views already examined, the call made to the owner and the unanswered question. Otherwise the handoff merely gives somebody else the job of reconstructing the incident.

An example of the mixed case, with arrival order retained across a handoff; spacing does not represent elapsed time.

The figure combines two kinds of repetition: several cameras observing at once, and one camera observing again. Time, property, camera and classification help decide which notifications belong together. They still need their own judgments. Opening one notification within the incident should make its neighbours available without marking them all as reviewed.

<a id="acknowledgement-has-a-limited-meaning"></a>

## Acknowledgement has a limited meaning

The neighbour has opened the alert. That tells us something, but not whether they can go to the shop. The operator has accepted the review. That does not give them authority to dispatch help. Even when both have responded to the application, the next action may still belong to nobody.

For the shop example, the difference looks like this:

| Participant | What they have done | What remains open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neighbour | Opened the alert and supplied a local observation | Has not agreed to respond |
| Monitoring operator | Taken on the review and called the owner | Still waiting for the owner's answer |
| Response participant | No acceptance recorded | Nobody yet confirmed to take the next action |

A useful handoff says which commitment someone has made. “Seen” cannot do the work of “I will continue from here.” Nor can a list of nearby contacts establish that any of them is available.

<a id="time-changes-the-problem-but-a-timer-does-not-solve-it"></a>

## Time changes the problem, but a timer does not solve it

While the operator waits for the owner, more cases arrive. Some need a quick check; others need a call or an image that has not finished loading. Counting all of that time as neglect would rush necessary work. Letting every active case wait indefinitely would hide the growing queue.

A review clock can make that wait visible and pass a case to someone else. Care's [clock example](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/an-interface-with-room-to-act/) follows the allowances for community, operator and manager review, including time spent loading evidence and making calls. Those are handling windows. They do not measure how long it takes help to arrive.

The next person must also be able to take the case. A manager on a call, an operator on a break and a disconnected workstation cannot absorb the same work as an available responder. And passing a timer's deadline cannot create authority: an escalation to emergency services needs an agreed route and their participation.

<a id="a-quiet-screen-can-mean-several-different-things"></a>

## A quiet screen can mean several different things

Suppose the operator has judged the scene suspicious. Another frame may show the person leaving, or reveal something more serious. Continuing to record matters. Asking the operator to repeat the same judgment for every frame may not.

That leaves two separate choices: whether to keep the observation, and whether to ask for attention again. Silence alone tells the next operator neither. The camera might still be recording while repeated alerts are suppressed. Another person might have taken over the watch. Or the connection might have failed.

Care's crime-classification rule illustrates the distinction: later notifications could keep joining the incident while further processing was suppressed for two hours. Suspicion had no equivalent cooldown because it could develop into crime. These choices make sense only if the person watching can tell which rule applies. A quiet screen must not look like evidence that the shop is safe.

<a id="the-outcome-can-exceed-the-application-s-account"></a>

## The outcome can exceed the application's account

The owner might call the police directly. A responder might arrive without updating the interface. An absent update then means the application does not know, rather than that nothing happened. The reverse error is just as consequential: a recorded request does not establish arrival or resolution. An [unanswered question](https://daostudio.in/collections/coordinating-emergencies/the-cases-that-are-still-waiting/) needs to remain available for someone to pursue.

Return to the shop at the change of shift. The incoming operator has two views, knows why the owner was called and can see that an answer is still due. They know who has offered to help and what remains unassigned. That is something they can continue. Without it, the system may keep delivering every alert correctly while everyone waits for somebody else.
