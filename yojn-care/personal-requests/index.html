YOJN Care is not a live emergency or dispatch service; this guide explains its personal interface. If you need help now, call your local emergency number.

You have pressed SOS. Did the request reach anyone, and has someone agreed to help? With one hand occupied and little attention to spare, you need to understand both. A moving indicator or a list of contacts cannot answer the second question.

SOS and High Alert start different reviews

SOS is a deliberate request for help unless activated accidentally. High Alert raises a concern for further assessment. The reviewer needs to know which arrived before deciding what to check; whether anyone has agreed to respond is a later question.

The emergency action stays visible. Distinct one-handed gestures for SOS and High Alert open a dedicated screen, where the person can follow the request. Groups, shared location and incident evidence help others understand the situation.

A request can begin without a camera view

A person asking for help should not need a camera image to make the request meaningful. A camera detection begins with an observation that needs interpretation. Care keeps these origins distinct, then follows what happens to each request:

State What the person needs to know
Sending The application is trying to reach the service
Received The request reached the service, but someone may still need to pick it up
Being handled Someone agreed to review or respond; dispatch and arrival are separate events
Correction requested A change or withdrawal needs to reach anyone already handling the incident

These distinctions matter even when a notification says “delivered” or “read”. A delivered message does not mean someone has taken it on. An opened message does not mean help is on the way.

When connectivity or setup blocks a request

If connectivity, a permission or incomplete setup prevents sending, the person needs to see that particular problem and reach the missing input or relevant phone setting. An urgent request should not send them through an unrelated administrative checklist.

The people view orders requests by urgency: unattended SOS, active SOS, unattended High Alert, then active High Alert. Family, friend and co-worker ordering applies within each group. A close relationship does not put a less urgent event ahead of someone still waiting for help. Nor does familiarity establish availability: who was notified and who agreed to act remain separate.

A correction needs to reach the continuing incident

Consider an accidental SOS. Cancelling before it is sent differs from correcting it after the service receives it. Someone may already be acting on the second request. Changing the local screen is not enough: the correction needs to reach those people, and the person cancelling needs to see whether it arrived.

Care's SOS flow carries the termination to the community separately from acknowledgement. The request's correction also needs to survive reconnection, so a delayed message cannot restore the earlier request without its update.

An interface with room to act examines these interaction choices. The monitor's view follows what happens when the request reaches another person.
